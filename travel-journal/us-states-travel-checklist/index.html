A Practical Guide to Visiting All 50 US States

Visiting all 50 US states is a classic travel goal because it is concrete, flexible, and achievable over many years. People pursue it on road trips, through work travel, and on family vacations. This guide covers how to count, how to plan, and how to keep the goal enjoyable.

What counts as a state

There are 50 states. The District of Columbia, where Washington, D.C. sits, is not a state, and neither are the inhabited territories: Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands. Many travelers keep a separate "bonus" list for D.C. and the territories rather than mixing them into the 50.

Decide your rule for "visited"

As with counting countries, the rule matters more than which one you pick. Here are the common ones:

  • Touched down: an airport layover counts. This is the loosest rule.
  • Left the airport: you went into the town or city.
  • Stayed a night: you slept in the state.
  • Did something there: you ate, explored, or saw something you would remember.

Choose a rule at the start and apply it everywhere, including to past trips.

Think in regions

Fifty states is easier to approach as clusters than as a single list. For example:

  • New England: six small states that can be combined into one road trip.
  • The Mid-Atlantic and Southeast: a long coastal sweep that can be done in stages.
  • The Midwest and Great Plains: often the states people have not yet visited, and well suited to long drives.
  • The Mountain West and Southwest: national parks and wide landscapes.
  • The Pacific coast, Alaska and Hawaii: Alaska and Hawaii are not part of the 48 contiguous states, so they usually need dedicated trips or flights.

The last few states on most people's lists tend to be the ones that are awkward to reach rather than unappealing, which is why a long-term plan beats last-minute improvisation.

Combine states in one trip

Some areas let you collect several states quickly because their borders are close together. Looking at a map for where three or four states meet can help you design an efficient loop, and driving between them is often more memorable than flying.

Keep a record you can see

A map that shades the states you have visited is motivating because the gaps are obvious. Whether you use a printed map with stickers or a travel-tracking app that highlights states, seeing the unfilled areas makes the next trip easier to plan.

Make each state mean something

The risk of a checklist goal is that it becomes a race. To avoid that, give each new state one memorable thing: a local dish, a state park, a small museum, a conversation with someone who lives there. Write it down. After fifty states, you will have fifty stories and not only fifty ticks.

Be realistic about the timeline

Few people finish quickly, and that is fine. Treat the goal as a background project that shapes where you go when you have a choice. Some people finish after decades, and a lot of the enjoyment is in the stretch between the first state and the last.
