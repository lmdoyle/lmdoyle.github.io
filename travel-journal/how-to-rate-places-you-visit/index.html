How to Rate the Places You Visit (and Make Your Ratings Mean Something)

Rating the places you have been sounds trivial, but ratings that are consistent become a surprisingly useful tool. They show you patterns in what you enjoy and make it easier to decide where to go next. Ratings that swing with your mood on a given day do not help much. Here is a framework for keeping them meaningful.

Decide what a rating is for

Before you start scoring, decide what you are measuring. Three common choices are:

  • How much you enjoyed it: a personal, subjective score. This is usually the most useful for planning your own future trips.
  • How good it objectively is: closer to a review for other people.
  • Whether you would return: a practical, decisive question.

Pick one meaning and keep it. Mixing them makes your ratings hard to interpret.

A simple five-point scale

A five-star scale is enough. Defining each level in advance keeps you honest:

  • 5 stars: loved it and would go back.
  • 4 stars: really enjoyed it, glad I went.
  • 3 stars: fine, nothing special.
  • 2 stars: disappointing, with a few redeeming moments.
  • 1 star: would not recommend or repeat.

Be willing to use the middle and the low end. If everything is a 5, the scale carries no information.

Separate the place from the circumstances

A stunning city can feel miserable during a storm, and a plain town can be wonderful because of the people you were with. Try to rate the place itself, but add a note about the circumstances so you can interpret the score later. Something like "5 stars, but largely because of the weather and the company" is valuable context.

Rate after a little time has passed

Your feelings immediately after a trip are colored by relief, fatigue or excitement. Rating a week or two later, once the details settle, often produces fairer scores. If you prefer to rate on the spot, revisit and adjust when the memory has settled.

Write one sentence of reasoning

A number alone is hard to interpret later. A single sentence such as "Walkable, great food, but crowded in summer" explains the score and records the thing you will want to remember. Over time, the comments matter as much as the stars.

Look for patterns

After you have rated a few dozen places, patterns emerge. Maybe your highest ratings are mostly coastal towns, or small cities with good food, or places with lots of walking and few crowds. Those patterns are the real payoff: they tell you what you like, which is better information than any generic "top destinations" list.

Use ratings to plan

When choosing your next trip, start with your top-rated places and ask what they have in common. Then look for destinations with similar qualities that you have not yet visited. Some travel apps use your ratings to suggest places you might like, which is a handy starting point as long as you treat the suggestions as ideas to investigate rather than guarantees.
