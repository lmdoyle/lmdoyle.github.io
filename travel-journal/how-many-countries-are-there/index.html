How Many Countries Are There? Why the Answer Depends on Who's Counting

It sounds like a simple question, but "how many countries are there?" has no single agreed answer. The number depends on which list you use, and travelers who track their visits need to pick a rule and stick with it. Here are the main ways of counting.

The United Nations count: 193 plus 2

The United Nations has 193 member states. Two further states are permanent observers rather than members: the Holy See (Vatican City) and the State of Palestine. Adding them gives the figure of 195 that many sources quote as "the number of countries in the world." It is a convenient, well-known benchmark, and it is the figure many travel trackers use as the denominator for a "percentage of the world visited" stat.

Why 195 is not the whole story

Some widely recognized places are not in that list:

  • Taiwan and Kosovo function as independent entities in daily life, but their status is disputed internationally and they are not UN members.
  • Territories and dependencies, such as Puerto Rico, Greenland, Hong Kong, or Gibraltar, are governed under another state's sovereignty to varying degrees but feel like distinct destinations when you visit.
  • Constituent countries inside a larger state, for example England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland within the United Kingdom, are often described as countries in everyday speech.

Some travel clubs and lists deliberately count territories and regions separately, which produces totals in the hundreds rather than under 200. Neither approach is wrong. They answer slightly different questions.

What counts as having "visited"?

This is where travelers argue the most. Common rules include:

  • Passed the border: any time on the ground counts.
  • Left the airport: a layover only counts if you went through immigration and out into the country.
  • Stayed a night: you slept there.
  • Did something real: you ate a meal, saw a sight, or otherwise experienced the place beyond transit.

The strictest rule is not better than the loosest. The point is to choose one that feels honest to you before you start counting, because it prevents second-guessing later.

Border crossings and microstates

Small states raise their own questions. Vatican City and San Marino are surrounded by Italy, and Liechtenstein sits between Switzerland and Austria. A short visit still counts under most rules, as long as you apply your chosen rule consistently. Some travelers also keep a separate list for "places I have been" and "countries I have been to" so that territories are recorded without muddying the main tally.

Picking a method for your own tally

  1. Decide whether you are counting UN members and observers only, or also territories.
  2. Decide what "visited" means (for example, you left the airport).
  3. Write both rules down at the top of your list.
  4. Apply them without exceptions, including to trips from years ago.

With a consistent rule, your count becomes a meaningful record of where you have really been rather than a number that changes with your mood.

A note on changing borders

Country lists change over time as states form, merge or change names, so any fixed total is a snapshot. If you care about precision, treat 195 as a reasonable approximation rather than an official, permanent figure.
